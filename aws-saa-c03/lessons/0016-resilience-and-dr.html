Resilience & Disaster Recovery

Design for failure, remove single points of failure, and match an availability/DR requirement to the right pattern — at the right cost.

Lesson 16 ~10 min Design Resilient Architectures · D2 · 26% Reliability pillar

The win

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to read a scenario, pull out its two numbers — how long can it be down and how much data can it lose — and snap to the correct disaster-recovery strategy. You'll know the four DR strategies cold, ordered by RTO/RPO and cost, and you'll stop falling for the trap of picking the most resilient (and most expensive) option when the question only asked for "cheapest" or "a few hours is fine".

Two numbers decide everything: RTO & RPO

Almost every DR question is really asking you to read two requirements out of the scenario. Get these straight and the rest is mechanical.

Memorize this distinction RTO (Recovery Time Objective) = downtime tolerance. How long can the system be unavailable after a disaster before it hurts? Measured forward from the outage.
RPO (Recovery Point Objective) = data-loss tolerance. How far back from the moment of failure are you willing to lose work? It maps directly to how often you back up or replicate. Measured backward from the outage.
Mnemonic: RTO is about Time-to-recover; RPO is about the last good Point (how much data). Lower numbers = stricter = more expensive.

If a hospital can lose at most 5 minutes of records, that's a 5-minute RPO — you need continuous/near-continuous replication. If it can be offline for at most 1 hour, that's a 1-hour RTO — you need infrastructure that can be brought up within the hour. The two are independent: you can have a tight RPO and a loose RTO, or vice versa.

Building blocks you already have

Two principles behind all of it Design for failure — assume every component will fail, and make recovery automatic. And remove single points of failure — anything you have exactly one of (one instance, one AZ, one NAT, one Region) is a SPOF until it's redundant. Multi-AZ kills the AZ-level SPOF; multi-Region kills the Region-level one.

The four DR strategies (the heart of this lesson)

AWS defines exactly four DR strategies. The exam loves them because they form a clean spectrum: as you move down the list, RTO and RPO shrink (faster recovery, less data loss) and cost rises. Learn the order and you can place any scenario.

The DR spectrum — highest RTO/RPO & lowest cost at the top, lowest RTO/RPO & highest cost at the bottom. Source: AWS DR whitepaper / Reliability pillar.
StrategyWhat's running in DR before disasterRTO / RPOCostRecovery action
Backup & RestoreNothing live — just backups/snapshots (ideally copied cross-Region)Hours (highest)LowestProvision infrastructure from scratch, restore data from backup
Pilot LightA minimal core always on — typically data replicated & critical services configured but app servers off/idleTens of minutesLow–mediumStart & scale up the dormant app tier around the live data core
Warm StandbyA full but scaled-down copy of the whole stack, always runningMinutesMedium–highScale the running environment up to production capacity, fail over
Multi-Site Active-ActiveFull production capacity live in both Regions, serving trafficNear-zero (lowest)HighestLittle to none — traffic just shifts off the failed site
Pilot Light vs Warm Standby — the most-confused pair Both keep the data replicated and something running in DR. The difference is what is running: Pilot Light = only the minimal core (data + maybe a tiny critical service); the application servers are switched off and must be started on disaster. Warm Standby = the entire stack is already running, just small; on disaster you only need to scale it up. Warm Standby recovers faster (no cold start) but costs more (everything is always on).
Trap Don't reflexively pick Multi-Site Active-Active because it's "the most resilient". It's also the most expensive and most operationally complex. If the scenario tolerates hours of downtime and stresses lowest cost, the correct answer is Backup & Restore — the most resilient option is the wrong one here.

The procedure for any DR question

  1. Extract RTO. How much downtime is acceptable? (hours → top of the list; near-zero → bottom)
  2. Extract RPO. How much data loss is acceptable? (this sets your replication/backup frequency)
  3. Find the cost/priority word. "Most cost-effective", "cannot tolerate any downtime", "minimal effort" — this breaks ties and is often the whole question.
  4. Place it on the spectrum. Match the tightest of RTO/RPO and the cost constraint to one of the four strategies; then pick the building blocks (Multi-AZ, Aurora Global DB, Route 53 failover, CRR) that implement it.

See it in action

Commit to an answer before you reveal each explanation — retrieval is what makes it stick.

Q1. Which DR strategy fits?
An internal reporting app is not business-critical. Leadership says it can be down for several hours during a regional disaster, and the team's overriding requirement is the lowest possible DR cost. Which strategy?
RTO = several hours (loose), priority = lowest cost. A loose RTO plus a hard cost ceiling lands you at the top of the spectrum: Backup & Restore (C) runs nothing in DR, so it's cheapest, and "several hours" is exactly the RTO it delivers. (A) is the most resilient and the most expensive — the classic trap when the question never asked for near-zero downtime. (B) and (D) keep resources running, costing more than the requirement justifies. Answer: C.
Q2. Which DR strategy fits?
A payment platform cannot tolerate downtime or data loss — RTO and RPO must both be near zero — and the business will pay for it. Which strategy meets this best?
RTO ≈ 0 and RPO ≈ 0, cost is no object. Only the bottom of the spectrum delivers near-zero recovery time: Multi-Site Active-Active (D) runs full production in both Regions, so a Region loss means traffic simply shifts with little to no recovery action. (A) means hours of rebuild. (B) requires a cold start of the app tier. (C) still needs a scale-up step, so it can't guarantee near-zero RTO. Answer: D.
Q3. Name the strategy from its description.
A company runs a complete but scaled-down copy of its production stack in a second Region at all times. On disaster they simply scale that running environment up to full capacity and fail over. What is this pattern called?
"Complete but scaled-down, always running, scale up on disaster" = Warm Standby (B). The whole stack is live in miniature, so recovery is just a scale-up — no cold start. The trap is (A) Pilot Light, where only the minimal core runs and the application tier is off and must be started. The tell: Pilot Light = minimal core launched on disaster; Warm Standby = full stack already running, just resized. Answer: B.
Q4. Which mechanism provides the failover?
A multi-Region web application needs traffic to shift automatically from the primary Region to the DR Region if the primary becomes unhealthy, with no manual intervention. Which AWS mechanism delivers this?
Automatic DNS-level cross-Region failover = Route 53 failover routing + health checks (A). Route 53 health-checks the primary and, when it fails, resolves the name to the DR endpoint automatically. The distractors don't cross Regions: an ALB is regional (B) and an ASG is regional/Multi-AZ (C) — neither spans Regions. CRR (D) replicates objects, not traffic, so it supports DR data but doesn't perform failover. Answer: A.

The one sentence to remember

Takeaway Read out the RTO (downtime tolerance) and RPO (data-loss tolerance), then walk the spectrum — Backup & Restore → Pilot Light → Warm Standby → Multi-Site Active-Active — choosing the cheapest strategy whose RTO/RPO still meets the requirement, and never pay for more resilience than the question asked for.
Primary source to read next (15 min): the AWS Well-Architected Reliability Pillar. Focus on the disaster-recovery objectives section — it defines RTO/RPO and the four strategies in AWS's own words, which is exactly how the exam phrases them.

Cost Optimization

When several designs all "work", which one is cheapest? Domain 4 rewards the architect who pays only for what's needed — across compute, storage, and the data-transfer line nobody reads.

Lesson 17 ~10 min Domain 4 · Cost · 20% Design for cost

The win

By the end of this lesson you'll spot the cheapest correct design when a scenario names cost as the priority. You'll know the three cost levers the exam pulls — compute (right-sizing, Spot, Reserved/Savings Plans, serverless), storage (S3 lifecycle, Intelligent-Tiering, gp3, multipart cleanup), and the one most candidates forget: data transfer. Find the lever, eliminate the option that's cheap but doesn't meet the requirement, and pick the rest.

Lever 1 — Compute: stop paying for idle

Compute is usually the biggest line item, and the question almost always hands you the access pattern. Match the pattern to the purchasing model:

Compute cost model by workload shape. Discounts are AWS "up to" figures (see the compute cheat-sheet).
Workload shapeCheapest right answerWhy
Steady, 24/7, predictable (1–3 yr)Savings Plan / Reserved InstancesCommit for up to 72% off On-Demand
Interruptible, stateless, flexible timingSpotUp to 90% off; tolerates 2-min reclaim
Spiky, short, event-drivenLambda (serverless)Scales to zero — no idle cost at all
Oversized "just in case" instanceRight-size (smaller / newer gen)Compute Optimizer recommends the fit
Unpredictable / short-termOn-DemandNo commitment — but never the cheapest for steady load
The On-Demand trap When a scenario describes a steady, always-on baseline and asks for lowest cost, On-Demand is the comfortable wrong answer. A commitment (Savings Plan / Reserved) is cheaper precisely because the load is predictable. On-Demand only wins when the load is unpredictable or short-lived.

Lever 2 — Storage: ride the cost waterfall

S3 cost optimization is mostly about not leaving cold data in Standard. The moves the exam tests:

  • Lifecycle rules auto-transition objects down the cost waterfall (Standard → IA → Glacier → Deep Archive) and expire them after N days — set it and forget it.
  • Intelligent-Tiering for unknown or changing access patterns: it auto-moves objects between tiers with no retrieval fee, so you don't have to guess.
  • Delete incomplete multipart uploads with a lifecycle rule — abandoned upload parts silently accrue storage charges.
  • gp3 over gp2 for EBS: gp3 is ~20% cheaper per GiB and decouples IOPS/throughput from size, so you stop over-provisioning capacity just to buy IOPS.
Cheapest ≠ correct (the retrieval-time caveat) Glacier Deep Archive is the lowest-cost class, but it retrieves in hours and bills a 180-day minimum. If the scenario needs data back "within minutes", Deep Archive fails step 2 (eliminate the non-functional) — use Glacier Flexible or Instant Retrieval instead. Always check retrieval time against the stated access need before declaring the cheapest class the winner.

Lever 3 — Data transfer: the hidden exam favorite

This is the lever candidates miss because it never appears on the instance bill — it appears on the network bill. Learn the shape of it:

Data-transfer cost direction. Egress to the internet is where the money leaks.
PathCostLever
Inbound from internet (ingress)Free—
Outbound to internet (egress)$$$Cache at edge with CloudFront to cut origin egress
Same-AZ, private IPFreeKeep chatty tiers in one AZ where HA allows
Cross-AZ$ each wayBe deliberate about cross-AZ chatter

Most-missed lever Data transfer / egress is the cost lever candidates forget. Two designs can be identical on compute and storage and differ by thousands of dollars on transfer alone. When a scenario mentions a private subnet pulling lots of data from S3 or DynamoDB, the cost answer is almost always a Gateway VPC Endpoint — it's free and removes the NAT data-processing charge. When it mentions serving lots of content to the internet, think CloudFront to cut origin egress.

See it in action

Run the Lesson 1 procedure: find the priority word (it's cost here), eliminate the option that's cheap but doesn't meet the requirement, then pick from what's left. Commit before revealing.

Q1. Which option does the procedure select?
Application logs are rarely touched after 30 days, but compliance requires keeping them for 7 years at the lowest possible storage cost. Retrieval, on the rare occasion it's needed, can take many hours.
Priority: "lowest possible storage cost" → Cost Optimization. Step 2 normally eliminates Deep Archive when fast retrieval is needed — but here the scenario explicitly allows hours, so the retrieval-time caveat doesn't bite. Deep Archive (C) is the cheapest class and meets every requirement. Standard (A) and IA (B) cost far more for cold data. Flexible Retrieval (D) is cheap but pricier than Deep Archive and buys speed the scenario doesn't need. Answer: C. (Contrast Lesson 1 Q1, where "within minutes" ruled Deep Archive out — always read the retrieval requirement.)
Q2. Which option does the procedure select?
EC2 instances in a private subnet pull large volumes of data from S3 all day. The architecture currently routes this through a NAT Gateway, and the NAT data-processing charges have become the biggest line on the bill. Cut the cost without losing S3 access.
Priority: cut NAT data-processing cost while keeping S3 access. A Gateway VPC Endpoint (B) is free, route-table based, and supports exactly S3 and DynamoDB — traffic stops traversing NAT, so the per-GB charge disappears and the subnet stays private. Moving to a public subnet (A) weakens security and still incurs internet egress. A second NAT (C) doubles cost, not cuts it. An Interface Endpoint (D) works for most services but bills hourly + per-GB — and for S3 the free Gateway Endpoint is the cost answer. Answer: B.
Q3. Which option does the procedure select?
A new data lake stores objects whose access pattern is unknown and likely to change over time. The team wants to minimize storage cost without guessing tiers or risking surprise retrieval fees.
Priority: minimize cost for an unknown, changing pattern with no retrieval-fee guesswork. That phrasing is the textbook tell for Intelligent-Tiering (D): it auto-moves objects between frequent and infrequent tiers with no retrieval fee, so you never guess. Standard with manual review (A) is operational toil. Forcing IA (B) risks retrieval/early-delete fees and the 128 KB minimum on small objects. One Zone-IA (C) sacrifices AZ resilience and still requires you to be right about the pattern. A custom Lambda re-tierer (E) reinvents what Intelligent-Tiering does automatically — with no retrieval fee and only a small per-object monthly monitoring charge. Answer: D.
Q4. Which option does the procedure select?
A core service runs a steady 24/7 baseline of compute that the company is confident it will need for the next 3 years. They want the lowest cost for that baseline.
Priority: lowest cost for a known, steady, multi-year baseline. A 3-year Savings Plan / Reserved commitment (A) delivers up to 72% off precisely because the load is predictable. On-Demand (B, D) is the trap — flexible, but the most expensive way to run an always-on baseline. Spot (C) is cheapest per hour but can be reclaimed with 2 minutes' notice, so it's wrong for steady, critical, always-on compute. Answer: A.

The one sentence to remember

Takeaway Match the workload's shape to its cheapest model — commit for steady load, Spot for interruptible, serverless to kill idle, lifecycle/Intelligent-Tiering for cold and unknown data — and never forget the data-transfer bill: free Gateway Endpoints and CloudFront are pure cost wins.

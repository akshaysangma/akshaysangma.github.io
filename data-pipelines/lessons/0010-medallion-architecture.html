Three layers, one story

The medallion architecture — bronze, silver, gold — is the canonical shape of a Databricks lakehouse pipeline.

Track: Databricks / PySpark ~11 min Hands-on: Databricks Free Edition Builds on: Lessons 8 & 9
The win today Every Databricks job description and every lakehouse design interview mentions the medallion architecture. By the end of this lesson you will be able to sketch the three layers, explain exactly what lives in each one, and defend your choices — the design question that separates candidates who have read about lakehouses from engineers who understand them.

The pattern at a glance

The medallion architecture is a data design pattern that organizes a lakehouse into three progressively-refined layers — bronze, silver, and gold — each typically a set of Delta tables.¹ Data flows in one direction: raw data lands in bronze, is cleaned into silver, and is aggregated into gold.

This is the canonical shape ELT takes in a lakehouse (recall Lesson 8): you load first — data arrives in bronze exactly as the source sent it — then you transform inside the platform, driving Spark compute to build silver and gold. Nothing is processed or modified before it lands. Because each layer is a Delta table (see Lesson 9), every layer has ACID guarantees, a transaction log, and time travel built in automatically.

The refinery metaphor Think of it as a refinery. Raw ore (bronze) is valuable to keep, but not directly usable. Refined metal (silver) is the standard form every team builds on. Finished product (gold) is what stakeholders actually touch. Each stage adds value — and because you keep every stage as Delta tables, you can always trace a gold number back to the raw ore it came from.

Bronze — the unedited record

Bronze is your landing zone. When data arrives from a source system — a CSV drop, a Kafka event stream, a database extract, an API response — it lands here exactly as-is: same schema, same values, same quirks and noise. Your only permitted additions are ingestion metadata: a load timestamp (_ingest_ts) and a source identifier (_source_file, _source_topic, or similar). No filtering, no type-casting, no deduplication.

Bronze tables are append-only in production. You never overwrite or delete raw records. The reason is concrete: if a bug in your silver job silently drops 5% of valid rows, your recovery path is trivially simple — re-run the corrected silver job against bronze. Bronze still has every raw row, untouched. If you had overwritten or discarded bronze, that path is gone.

Three reasons to keep raw data forever Replay — re-run any downstream layer any time a bug is found or business requirements change, without going back to the source system.
Audit — prove to regulators, auditors, or yourself exactly what arrived, when, and from where.
Recovery — silver and gold are derived artefacts. Bronze is ground truth. Lose bronze and you have lost the ability to reconstruct from scratch.

Silver — the single source of truth

Silver is where the interesting engineering work happens. You read from bronze and apply the transformations that make the data trustworthy and usable:

  • Deduplication — source systems routinely deliver duplicate events; remove them with dropDuplicates().
  • Filtering and validation — drop rows that violate business rules: null primary keys, negative amounts, dates in the future.
  • Type-casting — source files often treat everything as strings; cast dates to DateType, amounts to DoubleType, flags to BooleanType.
  • Enrichment and joining — join with a lookup table (product catalogue, country reference) to add context that downstream layers need.

The result is a conformed representation: one consistent definition every downstream team and pipeline can build on without re-doing the same cleaning work. Silver is the layer analysts and ML engineers reach for when they need row-level detail. It is emphatically not raw, and it is not aggregated.

Gold — curated for people and machines

Gold is the serving layer. You read from silver and produce tables that are immediately useful to a consumer without further transformation:

  • Business aggregates — daily revenue by region, monthly active users, campaign conversion rates, churn counts.
  • Dimensional models — fact and dimension tables shaped for a BI tool that expects a star schema.
  • ML feature tables — pre-computed features that a model needs at training or serving time, often stored in a Feature Store.

Gold tables are read-heavy and typically much smaller than silver — aggregation collapses many rows into few. A BI analyst queries gold and never needs to know what bronze looked like. Gold tables are usually rebuilt on a schedule; if the business definition of "monthly revenue" changes, you update the gold job and re-run it against the unchanged silver layer.

Layer count is a guideline, not a law Bronze/silver/gold is a useful mental model, not a rigid specification. Some teams add a "raw" layer before bronze for unpartitioned file drops, or a "platinum" layer for a particular ML domain. Others collapse silver and gold when the pipeline is genuinely simple. What matters is the intent behind each layer: raw/immutable, conformed/trusted, curated/serving. If your pipeline has two layers but they serve those three purposes cleanly, you are doing it right. Don't add layers to look architecturally sophisticated.

Layer summary

The three layers at a glance. Operations listed are the most common PySpark patterns at each layer — not an exhaustive list. Full API reference: Databricks PySpark docs.
Layer Purpose Typical PySpark operations Consumers
Bronze Land raw data as-is; append-only; add ingestion metadata only withColumn("_ingest_ts", current_timestamp()), write.mode("append") Silver pipeline jobs; incident replay; audit queries

Now build it

The code below traces five raw order events through all three layers. Every transformation choice mirrors a real engineering decision; the comments explain each one.

Platform note (Databricks Free Edition) Paste this into a Databricks notebook — spark already exists, so skip the SparkSession lines. The saveAsTable calls write to your default Unity Catalog schema. If you see a permissions or namespace error, prefix each table name with main.default. — for example, main.default.orders_bronze. Ask in the chat if you hit a snag. 
from pyspark.sql import SparkSession
from pyspark.sql import functions as F

# On Databricks: skip SparkSession — spark is already there.
spark = SparkSession.builder.appName("medallion-demo").getOrCreate()

# ── Tiny source dataset: five raw order events ───────────────────────────────
raw = [
    ("ord_001", "alice", "2024-03-01", 120.0, "USD"),
    ("ord_002", "bob",   "2024-03-01",  45.5, "USD"),
    ("ord_001", "alice", "2024-03-01", 120.0, "USD"),  # duplicate event!
    ("ord_003", "alice", "2024-03-02", 200.0, "USD"),
    ("ord_004", None,    "2024-03-02",  60.0, "USD"),  # missing customer!
]
schema = ["order_id", "customer", "order_date", "amount_usd", "currency"]

# ── BRONZE: land as-is, tag with metadata, write to Delta ────────────────────
bronze_df = (
    spark.createDataFrame(raw, schema)
    .withColumn("_ingest_ts",   F.current_timestamp())   # when we loaded it
    .withColumn("_source_file", F.lit("orders_20240302.csv"))  # where it came from
)
# append-only in production; overwrite here keeps the demo repeatable
bronze_df.write.format("delta").mode("overwrite").saveAsTable("orders_bronze")

# ── SILVER: read bronze → cleanse → write Silver ─────────────────────────────
silver_df = (
    spark.read.table("orders_bronze")
    .dropDuplicates(["order_id"])                        # remove duplicate ord_001
    .filter(F.col("customer").isNotNull())               # drop ord_004 (no customer)
    .withColumn("order_date",                            # cast string → DateType
        F.to_date(F.col("order_date"), "yyyy-MM-dd"))
    .drop("_ingest_ts", "_source_file")                 # strip ingestion metadata
)
silver_df.write.format("delta").mode("overwrite").saveAsTable("orders_silver")

# ── GOLD: read silver → aggregate → write Gold ───────────────────────────────
gold_df = (
    spark.read.table("orders_silver")
    .groupBy("customer", "order_date")
    .agg(
        F.sum("amount_usd").alias("total_revenue_usd"),
        F.count("order_id").alias("order_count"),
    )
    .orderBy("customer", "order_date")
)
gold_df.write.format("delta").mode("overwrite").saveAsTable("orders_gold")

# ── Inspect the result ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
spark.read.table("orders_gold").show()
# +--------+----------+-----------------+-----------+
# |customer|order_date|total_revenue_usd|order_count|
# +--------+----------+-----------------+-----------+
# |   alice|2024-03-01|            120.0|          1|
# |   alice|2024-03-02|            200.0|          1|
# |     bob|2024-03-01|             45.5|          1|
# +--------+----------+-----------------+-----------+
What to notice in the output Five raw rows entered bronze. The duplicate (ord_001 appeared twice) was removed in silver. The invalid row (ord_004, no customer) was filtered out. Gold has three clean rows — revenue we can trust. That progression from five noisy raw rows to three trustworthy aggregated rows is the architecture working as designed. If a bug were found in the silver logic tomorrow, bronze still holds all five originals.

Production bronze tables use mode("append") — you never discard raw data. The mode("overwrite") here is purely so the notebook reruns cleanly in a learning session without accumulating extra rows on every execution.

Check yourself

Commit to an answer before revealing — retrieval is what builds memory.

A raw CSV file has just arrived from a source system. Which layer receives it?
Bronze is the landing zone. Data arrives unchanged — the only additions are ingestion metadata (_ingest_ts, _source_file). Cleaning and deduplication happen in silver; aggregation in gold. There is no canonical "staging layer" before bronze in the standard medallion pattern — bronze is the raw stage.
A bug in your silver job silently dropped valid order rows. You have fixed the bug. How do you fully recover?
Bronze is append-only and holds every original row. Re-running the fixed silver job against bronze produces a complete, correct silver table — no source system access required, no guesswork. Rolling silver back (A) would undo other valid changes made after the bug and still leaves the silver table in a buggy state for the affected time range. Gold (C) has already aggregated away the row-level detail you need. Requesting a source extract (D) works but is slow, operationally painful, and entirely unnecessary — that is precisely what bronze is for.
An analyst needs a pre-built table of monthly revenue by region. Where does it live?
Gold is the serving layer — its purpose is exactly this: a pre-aggregated, business-level table curated for a specific consumer (the analyst, the BI tool). Bronze exposes raw, noisy data and forces every consumer to re-implement the same cleaning and aggregation. Silver has the clean row-level detail but is not the serving layer — asking every analyst to run their own groupBy on silver defeats the point of gold. A "platinum" layer (D) is a real pattern at some organizations, but it is not the standard answer for a single reporting table — gold is the right home.

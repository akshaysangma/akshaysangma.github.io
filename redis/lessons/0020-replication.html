Replication — How Replicas Keep Up

One master, N copies, all kept current over a live stream. The mechanism is simple and the one caveat — replication is asynchronous — is the thing that bites people during failover.

Phase 5 · Operations ~10 min Hands-on Builds on: RDB snapshots (L19)
Recall first Before the new idea: in Lesson 19 you learned BGSAVE forks the process and writes a point-in-time RDB snapshot to disk. Hold that — replication reuses exactly that machinery, except the RDB's destination is a socket to another Redis, not a file.

The shape of it

Replication is Redis copying one server's dataset onto one or more others, and then keeping them current. The source is the master (the leader); each copy is a replica (the follower)."Master/replica" is the current term; older docs and the SLAVEOF command say "master/slave." Same thing. The config directives were renamed too — replica-read-only was once slave-read-only — with the old spellings kept as aliases. A replica isn't a backup and it isn't a snapshot — it's a living mirror that trails the master by milliseconds.

Why you reach for it: read scaling (fan reads out across replicas), availability (a replica can be promoted if the master dies — that's Sentinel, L21), and as the substrate for sharding (each shard in a Cluster is itself a master with replicas, L22). Get replication right and the next two lessons are mostly orchestration on top of it.

You attach a replica with one command:

> REPLICAOF 127.0.0.1 6379   # "become a replica of that master"
OK
> REPLICAOF NO ONE           # detach → become an independent master again
OK

REPLICAOF returns immediately (O(1)) — it only schedules the sync, which then runs in the background. SLAVEOF is the deprecated alias for the identical behaviour. You can also bake it into the config (replicaof <host> <port>) so a server comes up already attached.

First contact: full resync

When a replica connects to a master it has never synced with — or has fallen too far behind to catch up cheaply — they perform a full resynchronization. The steps, in order:

  1. The replica sends PSYNC with no known history.
  2. The master starts a BGSAVE — it forks and produces an RDB snapshot (the L19 mechanism, reused).
  3. While that RDB is being made, every new write the master receives is buffered in the replica output buffer for that replica, so nothing is lost in the gap.
  4. The RDB is transferred to the replica, which loads it (replacing its own dataset).
  5. The master then streams the buffered writes, and from there a continuous stream of every subsequent write command. The replica is now live.

Older Redis wrote the RDB to a disk file on the master first and then streamed that file down to the replica. Modern Redis (since 7.0) defaults to diskless replication — repl-diskless-sync yes — where the master's forked child writes the RDB straight down the replica's socket without ever touching disk. That's a win on fast networks with slow disks (no disk write, no read-back).The replica side has its own knob, repl-diskless-load, controlling whether it loads the incoming RDB directly from the socket or stages it to disk first. Default is conservative (disabled) because direct-load can't fall back to the old dataset if the transfer fails mid-stream.

Full resync is not free A full resync forks the master, builds a whole RDB, and ships the entire dataset. On a multi-gigabyte instance that's real CPU, I/O, and network — and the copy-on-write fork can spike the master's memory (recall L19). You do not want a full resync every time a replica blips off the network for two seconds. That's exactly what partial resync exists to prevent.

Reconnecting cheaply: partial resync

Networks flap. A replica that briefly disconnects shouldn't have to re-download the universe to catch up on the few seconds it missed. Partial resynchronization handles this, and it rests on three things the master keeps:

  • The replication backlog — a fixed-size, in-memory circular buffer holding the most recent slice of the write stream (sized by repl-backlog-size, default 1 MB).
  • A replication ID (replid) — a random identifier for the master's current "history of writes."
  • An offset — a monotonically increasing byte count of everything ever streamed. Master and replica both track it.

On reconnect the replica sends PSYNC <replid> <offset> — "I last saw this history up to this byte." If the requested offset is still inside the master's backlog and the replid matches, the master replies +CONTINUE and streams only the missing bytes. No fork, no RDB, near-instant. If either check fails — offset already scrolled out of the backlog, or a different replid — the master falls back to a full resync.

Why the replid matters After a failover, the promoted replica becomes a new master and generates a new replid (keeping the old one as a secondary, so its own replicas can partial-resync across the handover). The replid is how a reconnecting replica tells "same history, I just missed a bit" from "this is a different timeline, I must reload." Offset alone can't distinguish those.

The one caveat: replication is asynchronous

This is the sentence to carry out of the lesson. The master does not wait for replicas before acknowledging a write to the client. The flow is: client sends SET → master applies it and immediately replies OK → then, asynchronously, the write propagates to replicas. The acknowledgement and the replication are decoupled.

The consequence is unavoidable and you must design around it: an acknowledged write can be lost. If the master confirms a write and then crashes before that write reaches any replica, and a replica is promoted, the new master never had it. The client was told "OK"; the data is gone. This is the price of asynchronous replication, and it is the defining property of how Redis HA behaves under failure. Synchronous, consensus-style replication is a different system with different latency — Redis chose availability and speed here, deliberately.

Bounding the risk: WAIT, and min-replicas

You can't make replication synchronous, but you can ask for bounded guarantees. WAIT blocks the calling client until a given number of replicas have acknowledged all writes issued so far, or a timeout elapses:

> SET order:42 confirmed
OK
> WAIT 1 100             # wait for ≥1 replica to ack, up to 100 ms
(integer) 1            # 1 replica confirmed within the timeout

WAIT is O(1) and returns the number of replicas that acknowledged. Crucially it is not consensus: if the timeout expires it returns however many acked (possibly fewer than you asked, possibly zero) and your write is not rolled back — there's no rollback in Redis. WAIT narrows the loss window; it does not close it. It buys you "this write reached a replica before I moved on," which is meaningfully stronger durability than fire-and-forget, but it is not a distributed transaction.

The complementary, preventive knob is on the master: min-replicas-to-write plus min-replicas-max-lag. With min-replicas-to-write 1 and min-replicas-max-lag 10, the master refuses writes (returns an error) unless at least one replica has pinged within the last 10 seconds. You trade availability for safety: rather than accept writes that might silently vanish, the master stops accepting them when it has no healthy replica to catch them.

Read scaling and stale reads

Replicas are read-only by default (replica-read-only yes) — a write sent to a replica returns -READONLY. That's a feature: it stops application bugs from creating divergent data on a node whose whole job is to mirror the master. Point your read traffic at replicas and you scale reads horizontally.

But because replication is asynchronous, a replica is always slightly behind — that gap is replication lag. Read from a replica right after writing to the master and you may get the old value: a stale read. For a follower count or a leaderboard, fine. For "did my own write land?", read from the master, or use WAIT, or read-your-writes routing. Know which of your reads tolerate staleness — that decision is yours, not Redis's.

Run this — stand up a replica and watch it sync Two terminals. Keep your master from earlier lessons on 6379; start a second instance as a replica on 6380: 
# terminal 1 — a second server on a new port
redis-server --port 6380

# terminal 2 — turn it into a replica, then inspect both
> redis-cli -p 6379 SET canary hello
OK
> redis-cli -p 6380 REPLICAOF 127.0.0.1 6379
OK
> redis-cli -p 6380 GET canary        # it synced the existing key →
"hello"
> redis-cli -p 6379 SET canary world  # write to the MASTER
OK
> redis-cli -p 6380 GET canary        # it streamed through →
"world"
> redis-cli -p 6380 SET canary nope   # try to write to the REPLICA →
(error) READONLY You can't write against a read only replica.
Now read the replication state on each side: 
> redis-cli -p 6379 INFO replication
role:master
connected_slaves:1
slave0:ip=127.0.0.1,port=6380,state=online,offset=...,lag=0
master_replid:<hex>
master_repl_offset:...
> redis-cli -p 6380 INFO replication
role:slave
master_host:127.0.0.1
master_link_status:up
slave_read_only:1
slave_repl_offset:...
The two offsets converging is the proof the stream is live. Watch master_link_status — up means connected and caught up; down means the link broke and a resync (partial or full) is pending.

Q1. Your master confirms OK to a client, then crashes a few milliseconds later before any replica saw that write. A replica is promoted. What happened to the write?
The client received a successful acknowledgement. The new master is now serving traffic.
B. Replication is asynchronous: the master replies before the write reaches replicas, so an acknowledged write can vanish on failover. (A — there's no journal a replica can replay that the master never sent; C describes synchronous/quorum writes, which Redis does not do by default; D — Redis has no rollback.) This single property drives every HA design decision in L21–L22.
Q2. A replica drops off the network for three seconds during light traffic, then reconnects. What does Redis do to bring it current?
C. A brief gap is the textbook partial-resync case: the replica's offset is still inside the master's replication backlog and the replid matches, so the master replies +CONTINUE and streams just the missing slice — no fork, no RDB. (A is a full resync, the expensive fallback you avoid here; B and D aren't how reconnection works.) If the backlog had scrolled past that offset, then it would fall back to A.
Q3. During a full resync, the master is busy producing the RDB. A client writes a new key to the master in that window. Where does that write go so it isn't lost? (Recall L19: the RDB is a point-in-time snapshot — it freezes at fork time.)
A. The RDB is frozen at fork time (L19), so writes after that moment can't be in it. The master buffers them per-replica in the replica output buffer and streams them once the RDB is transferred and loaded — then continues with the live stream. (B is impossible; the snapshot is already fixed. C — the master keeps serving writes normally. D would lose the write.)

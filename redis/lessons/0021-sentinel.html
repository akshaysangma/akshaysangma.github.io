High Availability with Sentinel

Replication keeps a copy warm; it does not promote it. Sentinel is the missing piece — the set of watchers that notice a master is dead, agree on it, elect a leader, and fail over without a human at 3am.

Phase 5 · Operations ~10 min Hands-on Builds on: Replication (L20)
Recall first From L20: replication is asynchronous, and a replica is read-only by default. So if the master dies, you have a warm copy — but who runs REPLICAOF NO ONE on it, and how do clients learn the new address? That gap is exactly what this lesson fills.

The job Sentinel does

Replication gives you a master and one or more replicas streaming its writes. What it does not give you is automatic failover: a dead master stays dead until something promotes a replica and re-points everyone at it. Redis Sentinel is that something.

What Sentinel is Sentinel is a set of separate processes — you run redis-sentinel (or redis-server --sentinel) — that monitor a master and its replicas, perform automatic failover when the master fails, and act as a service-discovery source so clients always find the current master. It is HA without sharding: your whole dataset still lives on one master.

Three responsibilities, worth separating in your head:

  • Monitoring — each Sentinel pings the master and replicas, and gossips with the other Sentinels.
  • Failover — when enough Sentinels agree the master is gone, they elect a leader who promotes a replica and reconfigures the rest.
  • Discovery — clients ask a Sentinel "where is the master for mymaster?" rather than hardcoding an IP. After a failover the answer changes, and well-written clients pick that up.

Sentinel is itself a special mode of the Redis server — same binary, different role. It speaks a subset of the Redis protocol, so you can talk to it with plain redis-cli. It is not a proxy: data never flows through Sentinel, only signalling and discovery.

SDOWN vs ODOWN — two words for "down"

Sentinel splits "the master is down" into two distinct states, and the distinction is the whole point of running more than one Sentinel.

A single Sentinel can only ever declare SDOWN — "as far as I can tell, the master stopped answering within my timeout." That is a local, possibly-wrong opinion: maybe that Sentinel got partitioned, not the master. So a lone SDOWN never triggers a failover.

When a Sentinel suspects SDOWN, it asks the others (SENTINEL is-master-down-by-addr). Once at least quorum Sentinels independently agree, the master flips to ODOWN — objectively down — and failover can begin.

The two-number subtlety (read twice) quorum is not the number that authorises a failover. It is only the number needed to detect ODOWN and start one. To actually perform the failover, a majority of the full Sentinel set must be reachable so they can elect a single leader. With 3 Sentinels and quorum 2: 2 agreeing flips the master to ODOWN, but if only those 2 are alive (the third is gone) that's still a majority of 3, so the failover proceeds. Set quorum 2 with only 1 Sentinel surviving and you get ODOWN that cannot complete — detected, never fixed. Quorum guards against false positives; majority guards against split-brain.

Electing a leader, then failing over

Once the master is ODOWN, the surviving Sentinels run a Raft-like leader election: per failover epoch, each Sentinel votes for one candidate, and a candidate that collects votes from a majority of all configured Sentinels becomes the leader for that failover. Only the leader acts — this is what stops two Sentinels from promoting two different replicas (split-brain).

The elected leader then:

  • Picks a replica to promote — preferring one that is reachable, has low replica-priority, the largest replication offset (most up-to-date), and the lowest run-id as a tiebreak.
  • Promotes it with REPLICAOF NO ONE — the chosen replica becomes the new master.
  • Reconfigures the other replicas to REPLICAOF the new master so they resync from it.
  • Updates its view so that get-master-addr-by-name now returns the new address, and publishes a +switch-master event.
Async replication means failover can lose writes Recall L20: replication is asynchronous. The promoted replica may be a few writes behind the dead master at the instant it died — those acknowledged-but-unreplicated writes are gone. Sentinel maximises availability, not durability. If you cannot tolerate that window, lean on min-replicas-to-write / min-replicas-max-lag on the master to refuse writes when replicas fall too far behind — and accept the availability cost.

Clients: discover, never hardcode

Here is the part application engineers get wrong. After a failover the master's IP changes. If your client has the old master's address baked into config, it will keep writing to a box that is now a read-only replica (or a corpse). A Sentinel-aware client instead bootstraps from the Sentinels: it asks SENTINEL get-master-addr-by-name mymaster, connects to whatever that returns, and subscribes to Sentinel's +switch-master pub/sub channel so it re-resolves the moment a failover happens.

Run this — a minimal Sentinel + discovery transcript Assume a master on :6379 with a replica on :6380 (you set that up in L20). Create sentinel.conf with the one directive that matters: 
# sentinel.conf — the monitor line is the whole config in miniature
# SENTINEL monitor <name> <master-ip> <master-port> <quorum>
sentinel monitor mymaster 127.0.0.1 6379 2
sentinel down-after-milliseconds mymaster 5000
sentinel failover-timeout mymaster 60000
sentinel parallel-syncs mymaster 1
Start three Sentinels (ports 26379/26380/26381) each pointing at this config, then ask one where the master is — this is exactly what a client does on startup: 
> redis-sentinel ./sentinel.conf --port 26379   # (run x3, different ports)

> redis-cli -p 26379 SENTINEL get-master-addr-by-name mymaster
1) "127.0.0.1"
2) "6379"                # the current master — resolve, don't hardcode

> redis-cli -p 26379 SENTINEL master mymaster   # full state, abridged
   ... "num-slaves" -> "1"  "num-other-sentinels" -> "2"  "quorum" -> "2"

# now kill the master process, wait ~down-after + election, ask again:
> redis-cli -p 26379 SENTINEL get-master-addr-by-name mymaster
1) "127.0.0.1"
2) "6380"                # Sentinel promoted the replica — new address, automatically
Watch a Sentinel's log while you do it and you'll see the state machine narrate itself: +sdown → +odown → +vote-for-leader → +failover-state-select-slave → +promoted-slave → +switch-master.
Time complexity Sentinel's commands aren't dataset operations, so "Big-O over N keys" doesn't apply — its cost is bounded by the size of the topology, not the data. SENTINEL get-master-addr-by-name is a constant-time lookup of cached state; monitoring is O(number of monitored instances + Sentinels) per cycle. The latency that matters here is the failover window: roughly down-after-milliseconds + election + promotion, typically a handful of seconds — tunable, never zero.

Deploying it: odd, ≥3, across failure domains

Because failover needs a majority, the size and placement of the Sentinel set is a correctness decision, not a capacity one.

  • Run an odd number, at least 3. Two Sentinels can never form a majority if one is lost — you'd be unable to fail over exactly when you need to. Three tolerates one failure; five tolerates two.
  • Spread them across failure domains (racks / availability zones). Three Sentinels in one zone gives you nothing if that zone is the thing that fails. Put one in each of three zones so a single-zone outage still leaves a majority.
  • Don't co-locate all logic on the data nodes. Sentinels are cheap; running them where clients live (or on dedicated tiny hosts) keeps their viewpoint independent of the data plane.
Sentinel is HA, not scale Sentinel keeps one logical dataset available by swapping which node is master. It does not shard: every key still lives on a single master, and total capacity is one box's RAM. When the dataset outgrows one machine — or write throughput outgrows one core — you need horizontal partitioning. That's Redis Cluster, next lesson, which shards and provides HA in one system. Reach for Sentinel when your data fits on one node and you only need it to survive that node dying.

Q1. You run 3 Sentinels with quorum 2. A network partition isolates 1 Sentinel; the other 2 see the master as unreachable. What happens?
The 2 reachable Sentinels both time out on the master and report it down to each other.
C. Two Sentinels meet quorum 2 (ODOWN reached) and form a majority of the 3 configured Sentinels, so they can elect a leader and complete the failover. (A confuses quorum/majority — 2 of 3 is a majority. B is what the majority rule prevents: only the single elected leader acts. D is wrong — a majority is present, so no waiting.)
Q2. A new hire hardcodes the master's IP in the app config "so it connects faster." After a Sentinel failover, writes start failing. What is the correct fix?
The promoted replica took over on a new address; the app is still pointed at the old, now-demoted node.
B. Clients must bootstrap from Sentinel via SENTINEL get-master-addr-by-name and subscribe to the +switch-master channel so they re-resolve after a failover. The master IP is not stable — that's the entire reason Sentinel exposes discovery. (A breaks writes since replicas are read-only. C and D just defeat the point of running Sentinel.)
Q3. Interleaving L20: after Sentinel promotes a replica, a customer notices a handful of writes acknowledged just before the crash are missing. Why is this expected?
The old master confirmed those writes to clients, then died before the promoted replica had them.
A. From L20: replication is asynchronous, so a replica can lag the master by some writes at any instant. If the master dies, those acknowledged-but-unreplicated writes are lost on promotion — Sentinel optimises availability, not durability. Tighten the window (never close it) with min-replicas-to-write/min-replicas-max-lag. (B and D invent behaviour Sentinel doesn't have; C is wrong — the replica keeps its replicated data, it doesn't reset.)
